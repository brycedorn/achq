# Building techniques

What experienced Minecraft builders do to make blocks read as architecture and landscape, gathered from the written
guides of the big build servers (WesterosCraft, Conquest Reforged), Minecraft.net's own tutorials and builders'
threads, and turned into rules for these designs. Each rule says why it works, because a rule followed without its
reason produces the "added detail for its own sake" look the guides warn against. `check` notes some of them (marked
**noted**); the rest are for the designer's eye.

## Depth

1. **No wall is one flat plane.** A wall in one block, one plane, over a large area reads as cardboard. Break it with
   things a real wall has: pillars or posts a block proud of the wall at the corners and between bays, a plinth, a
   cornice or a string course, recessed windows. **Noted:** a long, tall, unbroken run of one block on an outside wall.
2. **Depth has a reason.** Add depth where the structure would have it (an eave, a buttress, a window reveal, a sill,
   a post carrying a beam), not to break up a wall for its own sake, which looks arbitrary.
3. **Windows sit back.** Set glass a block into the wall, or use panes (thin, set in the middle of the block), so the
   reveal casts a shadow line that frames the window. Give it a sill (an upside-down stair or a slab under it), and
   shutters, a lintel or trim where the style has them.
4. **Doors sit inside the wall**, not flush with its face, for the same shadow.
5. **Roofs overhang.** An eave of 1 block, 2 on a large building, casts a shadow line that ends the wall and separates
   it from the roof; a roof that meets the wall edge-on looks unfinished. Build slopes from stairs and slabs, never
   full blocks.
6. **Partial blocks are the detailing kit.** Stairs, slabs, walls, fences, trapdoors, panes, chains and end rods add
   depth without taking a full block: upside-down stairs as brackets under eaves and sills, slabs as ledges, fences
   and walls as balusters, trapdoors as shutters.

## Shape

7. **No plain box.** Avoid symmetrical square footprints; an L, a T or a slight offset reads as a building that grew
   to its use. But every wing has a purpose.
8. **Silhouette first.** The outline against the sky is what reads from afar: vary roof heights, add a chimney, a
   dormer, a tower, a mast.
9. **Fit the land, don't fight it.** On a slope a building steps down, or its foundation does, so no earth shows under
   its walls and nothing floats. Set buildings into hillsides, under cliffs, over ravines.
10. **Foundations anchor.** A stone base (a plinth) a block or more proud of the wall, deep enough that no dirt shows
    beneath the walls on any side, not just the front.

## Material and colour

11. **A palette of 3 to 5 related blocks**, close in value and undertone, not one block and not a patchwork. Warm
    (wood) feels homely; stone and concrete feel cool. Contrast where the eye should go: trim against wall, roof
    against sky.
12. **Gradients, not splatter.** Where a surface varies, it varies for a reason: darker and rougher at the base, at
    corners, under windows (water staining), at eaves and drainage points; lighter and sun-bleached on exposed ridges.
    Blend in steps (drip) or in irregular clusters; never random noise, never rows or bands of clusters.
13. **Two woods, not one.** A main wood and a complementary one (oak and spruce, birch and oak, jungle and spruce)
    for frame against infill, or base against weathering.
14. **Materials suit the place.** Thatch and sod for the rural and the cold, slate for the wealthy and urban, wood
    for forests, whitewash and terracotta tiles round the Mediterranean, painted steel at sea. **Noted:** things out of
    place for the setting (open flame in space, snow somewhere warm).

## Interiors

15. **Rooms have a purpose**, and the space is divided into them: walls, partitions, a change of floor level, a rug or
    a beam line. One undivided room does not read as a place people live.
16. **Ceilings at least 3 high** where people stand; 2-high rooms feel cramped. **Noted:** rooms only 2 high.
17. **Every block earns its place.** Props tell a story when layered: a barrel with a mug on it, a desk with a lamp and
    a book, a workshop next to the hearth. Plain filler crates tell nothing.
18. **Light in layers**, warm and from believable sources (a hearth, lanterns, candles, windows), not a grid of the
    same lamp; softer, indirect light (hidden `light` blocks, glow under a shelf) for fill. **Checked:** no dark spot
    where mobs spawn.
19. **Ceilings show their structure.** Beams of stairs or logs spanning the short width of a room; rafters under a
    pitched roof, not the underside of the roof blocks.

## Landscape

20. **No straight line longer than about 7 blocks in anything natural.** Vary the outline of every layer of a hill
    or bank, and where each layer starts and ends.
21. **Trees are a trunk, branches and a canopy.** Taper branches (logs near the trunk, then fences and walls), vary
    their length, lean the tree and send most branches the way it leans, leave gaps in the canopy, mix shades of
    green. A solid ball of leaves looks artificial.
22. **The ground is full of small things.** Boulders, fallen logs, ponds, undergrowth and mixed flowers, in clusters,
    thicker in hollows and along water, thinner on exposed ground.
23. **Paths are worn, not laid**: darker in the middle where feet go, lighter at the edges, winding with the land.
    **Checked:** paths lead somewhere; no embankments.

## Ships

24. **The bow defines the ship.** Get the hull's shape right at the bow first, and taper it in plan and in section
    towards bow and stern.
25. **Curves from stagger**: angled planks and stepped stairs and slabs along the sheer, not blocky steps.
